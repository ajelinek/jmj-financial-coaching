## Personal Story - Jennifer and Adam's Journey

### Jennifer's Perspective

I had always been solely in charge of our finances. I paid the bills, decided how much to save, and how much to invest, but I had never written anything down on paper or shared the detailed numbers with Adam very often. Honestly, if I would share our savings numbers, his comment was always the same, "I was saving him into the poorhouse."

Despite always having enough money to pay our bills, we still had lots of money fights. The first year of our marriage was probably the hardest. The saver in me could not understand how he was coming to the marriage with a "beer bill" of all things. He had a good job. I also struggled because after paying for his loan, a loss on the sale of a house, a loss on the sale of a vehicle, the entire wedding, and some household items, my savings were drained. I had not been in this position since graduating college. My sole mission regarding finances was to rebuild our safety net.

Two years ago, I lost my job. I had worked in the IT field for 20 years. We had been a double income family for our entire marriage, 9 years at that point. We had previously talked about me being a stay at home mom, but we always thought we needed the extra income. I was freaking out, so I immediately sat down to make a budget. Who knew a budget could be such a blessing? Immediately, I saw a change in our relationship. We no longer had to wonder where in the world all of our money had gone. It was no longer me being a nag telling Adam we were spending too much money, eating out too much, buying electronics we did not need. It was the budget telling us that we were not sticking to the numbers we had both agreed to. Alleluia! Why didn't we do this 9 years ago!

After making a budget, we decided to make it work with one income. About a year ago, I bought Dave Ramsey's Financial Coach Master Training course. In July of last year, I completed my certification. Last fall we both took the Financial Peace University course. Money fights are the number one cause of divorce in America. I am passionate about helping couples save their marriage by reaching their financial goals and dreams.

### Adam's Perspective

All she wanted to do was save, save, save. We were newlyweds. I wanted to go out to eat (appetizer, dinner, dessert, and drinks), go to movies (with concessions), buy her expensive jewelry, buy a bigger TV, keep the thermostat at a reasonable setting, and so much more.

I could not understand what the problem was. We both had good jobs. We had enough money. I wanted to spend that money enjoying life. The beer bill she referred to was not a big deal. So I took out a loan to cover some living expenses while at the same time going out to the bars every night. I was used to living paycheck to paycheck, and in my mind I would always have some type of monthly payments.

We still need electronics. Do you know how old my laptop is? However, now we can plan for that purchase and add it to the budget. When we both discuss and agree to the budget, it feels good to be able to spend money without upsetting my wife.

## The Average American - Financial Reality

To give you an idea of America today… the average household income is $59K. The average consumer debt is comprised of $180K in mortgages, $50K in student loans, $29K in auto loans, and $16k in credit card debt. 78% of people live paycheck to paycheck. 7 out of 10 couples do not budget. 66% of Americans would struggle to pay for a $1K emergency. Debt has become normal. It has been marketed to us in so many forms and so aggressively since the 1960's that it requires a complete paradigm shift to even imagine living without a car loan or student loans or mortgages or credit cards.

In this day and age, with our fancy cars, big houses, and the positive spin we put on social media, it is hard to tell the broke people from the financially stable people. But if you gather up 10 couples, 8 of them would have a crisis if their next paycheck was late. Being broke is normal. Let's be weird!

Personal finance is 80% behavior and 20% head knowledge. That means where you are right now does not have to be where you end up, unless you give up. We have the power to change our financial future, and more importantly, to change our family tree.

We have all made mistakes with money. It is ok to glance back, but we have to focus forward. (Think about it like this – windshield vs. rearview mirror)

If you are sick and tired about feeling sick and tired about money, let's do something to change that! Let's break the credit card mindset and imagine being debt free and having savings. Debt gives you shackles and chains. Money gives you options, choices, and freedom. Figure out your why for getting out of debt and hold onto that reason.

### Understanding Personalities

Each of us is a nerd or a free spirit and a spender or a saver. The nerd loves to track things, wants details, wants information and a free spirit wants to focus on enjoying things. A spender as soon as they get money, they want to go do things with it and a saver loves the challenge of letting it build up, of seeing it, and watching it grow. We can be any combination of these (nerd spender, nerd saver, free spirit spender, free spirit saver).

When discussing finances as a couple, it is important to remember that we all enter this process with a past, a personality style, men vs. women, nerd vs. free spirit, and spender vs. saver. In the midst of all these differences, be sure to listen respectfully to your spouse's input and ideas.

## The 7 Baby Steps

### Baby Step 1 – Put $1,000 in a beginner emergency fund

The very first thing you need to do is build your starter emergency fund of $1,000. Think of it like a safety net, so when emergencies pop up out of nowhere (and you know they will), you don't have to use a credit card. If something goes wrong with the car, or you chip a tooth, or you need to replace the water heater in your house – your emergency fund will get you through without taking on any debt.

### Baby Step 2 – Pay off all debt using the debt snowball

Once you are prepared to avoid future debt, it is time to get rid of your current debt. Start by listing everything you owe (except your mortgage) from smallest to largest. This is called the debt snowball method, and you will use it to knock out your debts one by one.

Do not worry about interest rates unless two debts have similar payoff amounts – then you will list the debt with the higher interest rate first. The debt snowball is about reprogramming your behavior.

Attack the first debt on your list by paying as much as you can on it each month while making minimum payments on your other debts. When you've paid off the smallest debt, add what you were paying on that debt to the payment on your next debt and start attacking it. Your results will keep you motivated to dump all your debt.

Before you know it, you will be debt free!

### Baby Step 3 – Put 3-6 months of expenses into savings as a fully funded emergency fund

Now you are going to take your $1,000 emergency fund and expand it to 3-6 months of expenses. This will be your fully funded emergency savings, and it will protect you against life's bigger surprises.

Can you even imagine what life is like once you've completed this step? I mean, think about being debt free and having 3-6 months of expenses saved in the bank! That gives you peace of mind with your money, regardless of what life throws your way.

### Baby Step 4 – Invest 15% of your household income into retirement plans

The money you had been using to attack debt can now help build your future. In Baby Step 4, it is time to get serious about retirement, no matter how old you are.

Your goal is to invest 15% of your household income into pretax retirement accounts. Start by investing enough in your company 401(k) plan to receive the full employer match. Then invest the rest into Roth IRAs – one for you and one for your spouse if you're married. If your company doesn't offer a retirement plan or match your contributions, then go straight to the Roth IRA.

### Baby Step 5 – Save for your children's college

By this step, you have paid off all debts but the house and started retirement savings. Now it is time to save for your children's college expenses. Saving now will put you ahead of the game when your teens graduate from high school.

Look at 529 college savings plans. They are designed for saving and paying for college expenses, plus they have tax advantages.

### Baby Step 6 – Pay off your home early

There is one more debt standing in the way of you and total freedom from debt – your mortgage. Any extra money you can put toward your mortgage will help save you tens of thousands of dollars in interest. If you can make just one extra mortgage payment each year, you will shave 8 years off of a 30-year mortgage.

### Baby Step 7 – Build wealth and give

As you are continuing to invest and build wealth, now you get to be a tremendous giver! It is pretty awesome to have no payments – not even a house payment – and have so much income freed up to go and do incredible things for other people.

What would you do if you had this kind of freedom? Would you travel more? Invest in a business idea you have always had? Would you write a huge check to a cause close to your heart? Keep that dream in mind as you work your way through the Baby Steps.

**Note:** Baby steps 1, 2, and 3 must be done in order. Baby steps 4, 5, and 6 can be done simultaneously. Baby step 7 is the ultimate goal. To get there, we need a plan… a budget.

## Budgeting Myths and Reality

### Common Budgeting Myths

- I do not have time to budget.
- Making a budget is difficult and I hate math.
- Budgeting is boring.
- I can do a budget in my head.
- I budget by keeping track of everything I spend.
- A budget is too restrictive.
- There are always unexpected expenses, so why bother to budget?
- Budgeting means I can't go out to eat anymore. I hate cooking!
- It's not the right time for me.
- I make plenty of money… I don't need a budget.

### Building a Budget is Crucial to Your Success

A budget is people telling their money what to do instead of wondering where it went.

A budget forces couples to communicate and cooperate. A written plan, if actually lived on and agreed to, will remove many of the money fights from your marriage. A budget can heal a bad marriage and make a good marriage great.

The most important thing is for spouses to work together. It is not really about money, it is about communication. Budgeting requires 2 adults. You cannot parent a spouse.

Often times a budget has a "bread and water" connotation. It feels like a straightjacket. A budget is not any of that. It is simply a plan. For the spender, an agreed upon plan can feel like permission to spend money!

A written plan removes management by crisis. You learn to focus on the 4 walls (food, clothing, shelter, utilities, and transportation), get current on your bills, and then tackle paying off debt. Managed money feels like you are getting a raise.

## Budget Forms and Process

There are many different types of budget forms available. I have provided a copy of two of them - the Monthly Cash Flow Plan (a form that does your whole monthly budget) and the Allocated Spending Plan (a form that helps break your budget out by paycheck).

When you sit down to do your cash flow plan, you will be awful. It takes about 90 days to iron out the problems and be any good at budgeting. Let me say that again. It takes 90 days, or 3 months of working on your budget, for your budget to be any good.

Even while you are working out the kinks, your budget should have a zero balance. The total of all of the budgeted categories should equal your income. "Extra" money can be moved to savings or debt payments. EVERY dollar must have a name.

Things that mess up the budget – forgetting things and overcomplicating your plan.

You should budget, on paper, before the month begins, every single month for the rest of your life. Live on the budget. It is not a theory. Use the budget as your operating document unless you come back together and change the budget.

Balance your bank account. Remember the old school check register? Overdrafts are a sign of crisis living.

Once you have created your budget, plug the numbers into the EveryDollar app and then track each and every purchase as you make it. It literally takes 10 seconds to record an entry and you can immediately see the effect on your budget.

## Budget Committee Meeting

You should have budget committee meetings. There should be a meeting every month (probably weekly at the beginning). The decision making must be done by BOTH people. This is part of your marriage communication. The meeting should be a "safe place" to discuss financial worries, fears, stressors, etc.

### Rules for the Nerd

- It is not a weekend summit. You have 17 minutes.
- Insist that the free spirit mess with the budget.

### Rules for the Free Spirit

We also recommend as part of your budget committee meetings that you talk about your financial goals and dreams. Paint a vivid picture, together, of what life will look like in Baby Step 7. These steps will take a lot of hard work and sacrifice. Make sure you focus on the light at the end of the tunnel. Consider making a visual aid to track your debt free journey. As you change your behavior, and reduce your debt, it will feel good to track those milestones.

## Envelope System

As I mentioned earlier, personal finance is 80% behavior and 20% head knowledge. You have to pay attention. You have to be intentional with your money. You have to feel your physical money leaving your possession. It feels much different than the swipe of a credit card.

- Stop using credit cards
- Use debit cards and cash
- Convert some portion of your life to CASH so that you FEEL your money
- Use the Envelope System for a couple of categories such as groceries, restaurants, clothing, and fun money. For example, if your grocery budget is $600 for the month, at the beginning of the month take out $600 in cash. Put it in an envelope labeled groceries. Every time you purchase groceries, use the money in the envelope. When the money is gone, you are done buying groceries for the month. Stick to the plan.
